Keeping Your Loves Park Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush every year if their water is hard.

The age of your heater matters just as much. With regular service, a tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts acting up, like discolored hot water, rumbling, or moisture around the base, it's time to plan a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule costs less and is far less stressful than scrambling for a new heater with water spreading across the floor.

Small problems rarely stay small when they're ignored. Consider a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a quick, affordable repair, but once it's gone, the water starts eating the tank itself, and suddenly a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to keep our number in their phone and call at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves money and prevents a flooded utility room.

Hot Water Headaches Loves Park Homeowners Face

Water heaters in and around Loves Park deal with a predictable set of stresses. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling you may hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

We also see a lot of aging equipment. A tank-style water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, and near the end of that window the anode rod has often been used up, leaving the steel open to rust. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from failed thermocouples and draft problems to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Homes on private wells can bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: a thermal expansion tank on closed systems, a properly routed T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak could damage finished space, and earthquake straps in areas that require them. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most cost far less when caught early.
